Research

We are interested in evolutionary ecology. Particular areas of research include the evolution of sexual dimorphism, sexual selection and sexual conflict, and the evolution of life history traits. We tend to focus on insects, but often include other groups: past research has included beetles, true bugs, flies, odonates, fish, and amphibians. Our approaches centre on whole organism field and lab experiments, but also include quantitative genetics, developmental genetics, gene expression analyses, comparative studies, and the development of new theory.

Sexual conflict arises when the evolutionary interests of males and females diverge, particularly over mating rate and the outcome of copulations. Water striders (Gerromorpha) exhibit some of the most visible manifestations of sexual conflict: males have evolved a wide variety of structures used to grasp females and coerce mating, while females have evolved counter-adaptations, generating rapid diversification of reproductive morphology across populations and species. We study both the mechanisms underlying this antagonistic coevolution and the ecological context — predation risk, population density, environmental variation — that determines how intense sexual conflict becomes, comparing these traits across many populations and species to connect microevolutionary process to macroevolutionary pattern. A key part of this work links phenotypic evolution to its molecular and developmental underpinnings: in water striders, the Hox gene Ultrabithorax controls the characteristic leg-length ratios that allow these insects to skate on water, and another ancient developmental gene, distalless (dll), controls the sexually antagonistic modification of male grasping antennae. Genes like these, first known for patterning body segments and limbs, have been repeatedly co-opted to generate novel, sexually antagonistic traits across different insect lineages.

Sexual dimorphism — differences between males and females in morphology, physiology, behaviour, and life history — is ubiquitous in animals yet highly variable across taxa. We investigate how natural and sexual selection, and intralocus sexual conflict (a genetic tug-of-war that occurs when the same gene has different fitness optima in males and females), interact with local ecology to produce the extraordinary range of sex differences seen in nature. We also ask whether the ecological pressures driving sexual dimorphism contribute to the formation of new species: predation risk, for example, increases dispersal rates among habitats in a dose-dependent way, and work with experimental newt populations and other systems explores how such dispersal can promote or constrain diversification.

A separate thread examines how a population's history of selection shapes its evolutionary present. We study how past bouts of inbreeding, environmental change, or relaxed selection shape the genetic variance available for fitness, how prior selective regimes influence the outcome of sexual selection, and how selective history and current environment together shape the sex-biased transcriptome — the pattern by which many genes are expressed differently in males and females — using experimental populations tracked over many generations.

Life history traits (age and size at maturity, reproductive effort, lifespan) are fundamental targets of natural selection yet show remarkable variation within and among species. We develop and test theory on how trade-offs among life history traits evolve in different ecological contexts, and how sexual conflict influences the evolution of life history strategies in each sex. Recent collaborative work examines how the pace-of-life, epistatic gene interactions (where the effect of one gene depends on another), and ecology together shape the maintenance of genetic variation in life-history genes across populations.

Water striders (family Gerridae and related semi-aquatic bugs, order Hemiptera) are our primary model system: they are visually conspicuous, easy to culture, amenable to quantitative genetic and developmental approaches, and show striking variation in sexually antagonistic traits between species. We use both laboratory model species and field-collected samples from natural populations across North America, and also work with other taxa, including ambush bugs (Phymata), beetles, odonates, and amphibians, when they offer specific advantages for testing a particular hypothesis.
